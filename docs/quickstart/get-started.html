Open Design · Get started

Describe it. Watch it get built.

Open Design lets you make a real dashboard, pitch deck, or web page just by describing it in plain English — then refine it by chatting. No design skills. No code.

✓ Runs on your own computer ✓ Your files stay yours ✓ Talk to it like a person

First, what is this thing?

Open Design is a small app that runs on your own computer. You use it through a normal web page — inside its own window, or in your browser at a local address like localhost:7456. Nothing goes online unless you choose to share it.

🙂 You Type what you want, in plain English.
→you describe
🖥️ Open Design The app on your computer, shown in a browser window.
→it asks an AI
✨ A finished design Built live in front of you — to keep, share, or hand off.

Behind the scenes it talks to an AI model (like Claude) to do the actual designing — but you never touch code. You just describe, look, and refine. Think of it as a designer on call who works instantly and never gets tired of edits.

1 Start it up

Open the app.

The easy way: download the Open Design desktop app (the zero-config option), install it like any other app, and open it. You’ll land on the screen on the right.

Running it from a developer setup? Just open your browser to the address it shows you (often localhost:7456).

The first time, Open Design looks for an AI helper on your computer and connects automatically — you can see it in the corner (Local CLI · Claude Code). If it can’t find one, open Settings once and paste an API key; it walks you through it.

See the + Create button? That’s all you need to start a project.

2 Tell it what you want

Type a few sentences. Be specific.

Pick what kind of thing to make (a Dashboard, a Slide deck, a landing page…) and a look (the visual style), then describe it and press Send. It may ask a couple of quick questions first — answer them, or skip. Here are two you can copy and paste right now:

A business dashboardfor a real meeting
Choose: Type · Prototype + a clean Design system
Create a sales dashboard for “Bean & Bloom,” a coffee subscription business. Put four big numbers across the top: monthly revenue $48,200, active subscribers 1,240, churn rate 3.1%, and average order value $38.90. Below them, add a line chart of revenue for the last 6 months (Jan–Jun, trending up) and a bar chart of the top 5 products by sales. Then a small table of the 5 most recent orders with customer name, plan, and amount. Clean, modern look with a warm green accent and lots of white space.
A client pitch deck6 slides, presentation-ready
Choose: Type · Slide deck + an editorial Design system
Make a 6-slide pitch deck for “Northwind Studio,” a boutique branding agency, presenting to a new restaurant client called “Cedar & Salt.” Slides: (1) cover with the studio name and one confident promise, (2) the challenge the restaurant faces, (3) our approach in three steps, (4) a moodboard slide describing warm, rustic, modern visuals, (5) timeline and pricing with three packages, (6) a thank-you slide with contact details. Warm, editorial style with big type and generous spacing.

You type these into the box that says “Describe the design you want…” at the bottom of the chat. That’s it — no special syntax.

What happens after you hit Send

The screen splits in two. You chat on the left; your design builds itself on the right, live, as the AI works.

3 Make it yours — just keep chatting

Don’t start over. Ask for changes.

This is the part that makes it feel like magic. You don’t edit anything by hand — you just say what you want different, and the design updates in place:

You: Make the revenue number the biggest thing on the page.
You: Change the green accent to navy blue.
You: Add a “refunds this month” card next to churn.
You: The deck feels busy — fewer words per slide, more space.

When it looks right, click Save to disk (or Share). You get a real web page file you can open in any browser, email to a client, or hand to a developer.

The secret to a great result

Vague in, vague out. The more real detail you give, the better it gets. A good prompt has three things:

1
Who it’s for“a coffee subscription business,” “a new restaurant client.” Name the audience and the situation.
2
The real detailsActual numbers, the slides you need, the columns, the sections. Real content beats placeholders.
3
The vibe“clean and modern,” “warm and editorial,” “calm green accent.” Tell it how it should feel.
